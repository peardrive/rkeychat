# Make the revocation cite the log, not the clock

**Answer first.** A room without a server can never learn *when* a post was written, only what it provably came *after* (the hashes it cites) and what provably came *after it* (honest records citing its hash, a key that stopped existing, or a countersignature). Every timestamp field in every surveyed system, from Git `--date` to Matrix `origin_server_ts` to Nostr `created_at` to rKeyChat's `ts`, is the writer's claim, and admitting a record because its key "was trusted at `ts`" admits exactly the backdating a revoked key wants. The best fix for rKeyChat is structural and costs 1–3 hash references per record: make every record cite the latest record hashes it has seen, make the owner's `revoke` entry cite the room's current heads, and redefine "before the revocation" as "in the causal past of the revocation entry." That rule is decidable with any number of dishonest members and survives days-long partitions; its one false negative, an honest pre-revocation post delivered late, is the irreducible price of having no trusted clock and belongs to display policy, not to `#admits`. Add a periodic owner checkpoint (a ~112-byte signed Merkle root, countersigned by members at 64 bytes each) for peers who were offline, and optionally daily epoch session keys for forward security against a stolen *posting* key. "Revoke, re-sign the whole history under a live key, forget the old key" has no precedent in any shipped or proposed system, launders an attacker's injected posts, destroys the evidence a dispute needs, and is dominated by one ~150-byte attestation over a checkpoint. "The owner records when it saw each post" is sound as a *witness*, never as canonical truth: the room root signs online from `config.json` today (audit M3), so an owner receipt is worth exactly the root's security at that moment and nothing once the root is what was stolen.

## Only three things produce a not-after bound, and none is a clock

The taxonomy below is this report's synthesis, not any single source's. A **not-before** bound (created after T) is free: embed any value that first existed at T. It refutes forward-dating and is useless against backdating, because a thief embeds an old value. A **not-after** bound (existed by T) can only come from an action taken at T by something the thief did not control at T: another party's signature over the digest (receipt, witness, CT SCT, checkpoint), a key that no longer existed after T (forward-secure signatures), or sequential work observed at T (VDFs). Everything else is a hint.

Logical clocks fail completely: Lamport, vector, HLC and interval-tree clocks are self-asserted counters, and a Byzantine writer that reuses a sequence number makes two honest peers "believe that they have delivered the same set of updates because their version vectors are identical, even though they have in fact delivered different updates" ([Kleppmann 2022 §2.3](https://martin.kleppmann.com/papers/bft-crdt-papoc22.pdf)); by HLC's Theorem 3 one far-future value is adopted by every honest node downstream ([Kulkarni et al.](https://cse.buffalo.edu/tech-reports/2014-04.pdf)). Hash-linked DAGs are the first mechanism that yields evidence a liar cannot fabricate, but their timestamp fields are still attacker-controlled: Matrix's authors call `depth` "untrustable" and `origin_server_ts` something "an attacker can set to arbitrary values", the documented v1 attack is backdating by DAG position to evade bans ([MSC1442](https://github.com/matrix-org/matrix-spec-proposals/blob/main/proposals/1442-state-resolution.md)), and the state-reset class was still producing CVEs in August 2025 ([Project Hydra](https://matrix.org/blog/2025/08/project-hydra-improving-state-res/)). Nostr relays bound ingestion only against their own wall clock, by default +15 minutes and −3 years at strfry ([strfry.conf](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hoytech/strfry/master/strfry.conf)).

The quorum tier buys a timestamp a liar cannot move, at a price this room cannot pay. Bracha reliable broadcast costs (n−1)(2n+1) protocol messages per chat message, **44 at n=5 and 4,949 at n=50**, and a 50-member room needs 34 responsive parties to deliver anything ([Auvolat et al. 2021](https://adnab.me/files/papers/2021-TCS-BCO-Broadcast.pdf)). Hashgraph's consensus timestamp, the median of famous witnesses' received times, is the one surveyed mechanism where a backdated event simply gets the honest median, but it assumes "more than 2n/3 of which are honest" and participating, so members offline for days stall every new timestamp ([Baird 2016](https://www.swirlds.com/downloads/SWIRLDS-TR-2016-01.pdf)). Hash-graph causal broadcast without quorums tolerates any number of Byzantine members and arbitrary offline periods at 1–3 predecessor hashes per message, because dropping sequence numbers "removes the need for any quorum votes" ([Kleppmann 2022](https://martin.kleppmann.com/papers/bft-crdt-papoc22.pdf)); it delivers causal order and no agreed timestamp.

| Mechanism | Not-after bound it gives | Proves to whom | Cost at room scale (derived) | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logical clocks (Lamport/VC/HLC/ITC) | none | — | 8 B to ~2 KB/msg | always, against a liar; forward-dating is contagious |
| Hash DAG references (`prev`/heads) | "before any honest record that cites it" | anyone, transferable | 32–96 B/msg; heads+Bloom sync, ~2 RTT | an unreferenced orphan looks like an honest late delivery |
| Revocation entry citing room heads | "before revocation" = in its causal past | anyone | 32–96 B once | honest pre-revocation post delivered late is classed "after" |
| Receipt by another key (KERI witness, SCT, owner) | "before the receipter signed" | anyone, if receipter's log is anchored | ~108 B/receipt; 10k msgs × 3 receipters ≈ 3.2 MB | receipter offline, compromised, or receipts not propagated |
| Signed checkpoint (Merkle root) + inclusion proof | "before checkpoint C_t", batched | anyone, incl. offline peers | ~112 B/checkpoint; proof at N=10,000 is 14 hashes = 448 B | window between checkpoints; forked views unless countersigned |
| Forward-secure / epoch-rotated posting key | "before the signer advanced past epoch i" | anyone who trusts the deletion discipline | 96 B/msg inline or one 64-B cert per epoch; 2 verifications/msg | primary key stolen (thief certifies a new "epoch 3" key); signer forgets to advance |
| Hashgraph median timestamp | real timestamp one liar can't move | anyone | ~170 B/event, continuous gossip | ≤2n/3 online: no new timestamps |
| Bracha / BCO broadcast | none (order only, no equivocation) | — | 44–4,949 msgs per chat msg | offline members stall delivery |
| TESLA, VDFs | receiver-only / needs an observer of the output | one receiver / anyone with observer | n/a / a dedicated core forever | "TESLA alone cannot support non-repudiation" ([RFC 4082](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4082.html)); no JS VDF |

CPU is not the constraint: noble pure-JS ed25519 signs in **137–283 µs and verifies in 500–665 µs on an Apple M4** (the two noble READMEs differ: [noble-ed25519](https://github.com/paulmillr/noble-ed25519), [noble-curves](https://github.com/paulmillr/noble-curves)); libsodium-WASM signs in 21 µs ([00f.net](https://00f.net/2026/06/17/ed25519-wasm-signatures/)). A few thousand records at two verifications each is seconds, once.

## Re-signing history has no precedent and launders the attacker's posts

Across Hypercore/Autobase, Keybase, SSB, Matrix, Signal, Nostr, Session, Briar, Delta Chat, SimpleX, KERI, DID Core, TUF, Sigstore, Debian and atproto, **no system re-signs a revoked key's past records one by one**, and none of the Nostr migration PRs proposes it. Shipped behaviour falls into four buckets: old signatures stay valid judged against key state *at log position* (Keybase: "old links remain valid even if their signing keys are revoked later", [Keybase](https://book.keybase.io/docs/server); KERI; Autobase, whose `lib/linearizer.js` never un-yields a removed writer's indexed nodes); the trust *verdict* decays while the message stays (Matrix's shields, which MSC4147 calls unsatisfactory because "a message should be either trusted or not", [MSC4147](https://github.com/matrix-org/matrix-spec-proposals/blob/main/proposals/4147-including-device-keys-with-olm-encrypted-events.md)); the identity is abandoned (SSB: "generate a new identity"; Nostr PR #1056: "No event, past and future, from this key can be trusted anymore", [PR #1056](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/pull/1056)); or the account resets as "a cryptographic stranger" (Keybase). Git is the only place wholesale per-item re-signing happens, by individuals, and Git 2.52 moved the other way with `strip-if-invalid` because "Having invalid signatures in a rewritten repository could be confusing" ([git c20f112e](https://code.googlesource.com/git/+/c20f112e5149d1bd0d4741c4b28a65f81318309a)). The two cases that look like re-signing, Debian re-signing live Release files and atproto's rotation commit that "does not need to update the data CID link" ([atproto](https://atproto.com/specs/repository)), are one signature over a root, not N over items.

The idea fails on logic before cost. After a compromise both owner and thief hold the old key, so "re-sign whatever is in the log" vets nothing: the thief's pre-staged posts are laundered into account-vouched records, the attack PR #1056 describes (an attacker who "pre-stages DMs and public posts to friends, then sends the kind:18 at an opportune time"). Re-signing only up to a checkpoint H the owner confirms carries the same information as one attestation over H. "Forget the old key" then breaks the invariant every precedent leans on: a peer offline during the rewrite returns holding records under K with no revocation to say they are superseded (Debian's 2006 installer "only know[s] about the 2006 key", [SecureApt](https://wiki.debian.org/SecureApt)); old clients fail the way Atom 1.63 could only be removed, not re-signed ([GitHub](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/action-needed-for-github-desktop-and-atom-users/)); and the one artefact that distinguishes "Guy's phone said this on Tuesday" from "Guy's account later said Tuesday's log is fine" is the thing deleted. You must remember K to forget K.

Cost is secondary but real: ~110–140 B per message for signature-only patches (13 MB per peer at 100k, 0.65 GB aggregate across 50 peers), whole records if the id covers the signature. rKeyChat's `id` is 12 random bytes (`src/proto.js:25`, verified), so ids would survive, but on an append-only log replacing record 37 of N means truncating and re-appending everything, a new log every peer re-replicates, with days-offline peers returning on a fork (inference from `core.truncate` semantics, [hypercore README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/holepunchto/hypercore@main/README.md)).

| | Per-item re-sign | One attestation over Merkle root | Re-sign then forget key K |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bytes | ~13–200 MB / 100k | ~150 B | as per-item plus a new log |
| What it proves | account vouched for each record at T_resign | account vouched for set {ids ≤ H} at T_resign | nothing about origin |
| Attacker's injected posts | laundered if "whatever is in the log" | excluded iff H precedes compromise | laundered, evidence destroyed |
| Late peer | needs every patch and the revocation | needs one event and the revocation | sees conflicting signatures, no rule |
| Dispute later | answerable only if old sig kept | fully answerable | unanswerable |

**The cheaper equivalent**: one forward-dated statement by the live key, "I stand behind everything the revoked key signed up to checkpoint H; nothing after." That is Keybase's revoke link, KERI's superseding rotation, Debian's "rollback to last known good" and Adobe's "code signed after July 10, 2012 (00:00 GMT)" cutoff ([APSA12-01](https://www.adobe.com/mena_ar/support/security/advisories/apsa12-01.html)) in one record. rKeyChat's `compromised` revoke already carries `since` (an rKey `revoke` field; audit §1); the defect is that `since` is a time compared against self-asserted `ts`, so the thief writes `ts < since`. Make it a hash and the pattern is already there.

## Owner receipts are a witness, not canonical truth

An owner-signed receipt `Sig_owner(H(m), seq, t)` proves the owner held `H(m)` when it produced receipt `seq`; to everyone else that is a not-after bound *in the owner's timeline*, and it becomes a wall-clock bound only through evidence about when `seq` happened: members' records citing the receipt, the owner's later posts, or an external anchor. KERI gets this right by carrying no timestamps in receipts at all: ordering is "determined by its previous event digest and sequence number", "the first seen version of an event always wins", and the controller "accepts accountability for an event when any subset M of the N witnesses confirm that event" ([KID0010](https://identity.foundation/keri/kids/kid0010.html)). Certificate Transparency makes the receipt a promise with a deadline and treats two conflicting tree heads as proof of misbehaviour found by gossip ([RFC 6962 §7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962.html)), while RFC 9162 concedes a log showing different views to different clients can evade audits ([RFC 9162](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9162.html)).

Three failure modes follow. **Owner offline**: no receipt until it returns; CT's answer is the deadline, KERI's is M-of-N, and for a 5–50 member room the owner's receipt should be one of k, every receiving member being a receipter. **Owner compromised**: the thief mints backdated receipts; the defences are first-seen (a later version "cannot produce an alternate version of the event that could supplant the First-seen version", [ToIP KERI spec](https://trustoverip.github.io/kswg-keri-specification)) and chaining receipts into the owner's own sequence-numbered log so an early-dated receipt late in the sequence is self-evidently inconsistent. This is not hypothetical here: audit M3 has the root signing online from the daemon's `config.json` with no rotation, so a compromised MacBook is a compromised root and its receipts are canonical for nothing. **Receipts not propagating**: CT ships the SCT with the certificate and KERI replicates the KERL; the analogue is appending receipts or batched checkpoints to the room log so every peer stores them.

Batched into a checkpoint the receipt becomes cheap and offline-friendly: {size, root, time, sig} ≈ 112 bytes, an inclusion proof at **N = 10,000 is 14 hashes = 448 bytes**, and a consistency proof between checkpoints is at most ceil(log2 n)+1 nodes ([RFC 6962 §2.1.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962.html)). A peer back from three days away who receives (record, proof, checkpoint C_t) learns the record existed by t as attested by the owner; with members' countersignatures on C_t (64 bytes each, ≤3.2 KB per checkpoint at 50 members, ≤28 MB per year hourly) the bound stops depending on the owner's later honesty. This is also the one interface with the other agent's topic: anchoring the checkpoint root in OpenTimestamps turns an owner-asserted bound into a third-party one, once per checkpoint rather than per message.

## Judge a doubtful post by a window and its witnesses, not a score

No source offers a weighted trust score for a message from a later-compromised key; the notes report the absence rather than invent one. What exists are binary gates: chain position (Keybase replays "to check that each new signature was valid at the time it was made", [moderncrypto](https://moderncrypto.org/mail-archive/messaging/2017/002342.html)), a date (Adobe), a time-locked override (did:plc's 72 hours, [atproto #279](https://github.com/bluesky-social/atproto/issues/279)), social confirmation (Nostr #1056: did trusted contacts move their `kind:3` lists), and "any fork is proof of compromise" (KERI). DID Core names the trap rKeyChat is in: revocation is "not retroactive" only if signing time can be established, otherwise "someone could use a revoked key with a simulated past date" ([DID Core §9.8](https://www.w3.org/TR/did-core/#verification-method-revocation)). The tiers below are this report's synthesis; the evidence types are cited above, the ordering is inference.

| Tier | Rule | Evidence type | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Record is in the causal past of the author's `revoke` entry (the entry, or a record it transitively cites, cites the record's hash) | structural; any number of liars | trusted as before revocation |
| 2 | Record has an inclusion proof against a checkpoint countersigned by ≥M members, and that checkpoint precedes the revocation in the DAG | transferable receipt | trusted |
| 3 | Record is signed under an epoch key whose epoch ended (per the author's later records) before the compromise point | forward security; depends on deletion discipline | trusted, marked "by epoch" |
| 4 | Record arrived after the revocation, claims `ts` before it, and nothing honest cites it | none; indistinguishable from an honest late delivery | quarantine with "received after revocation"; owner can whitelist a set with one attestation |
| 5 | Record cites a hash created after the revocation, or the author equivocated (two records, same `prev`), or it lies past the `until` checkpoint | structural | rejected; equivocation is itself evidence of compromise |

Two rules fall out. `ts` survives only as a display tiebreaker among records the DAG leaves concurrent, as Matrix v2 demoted `origin_server_ts` to a last resort behind auth-DAG ordering ([Matrix v11](https://spec.matrix.org/latest/rooms/v11/)), plus a Nostr-style local window (reject `ts` more than ~15 minutes ahead of arrival) against forward-dating. And tier 4 is the honest cost of partition tolerance: a member offline for three days legitimately produces a post whose `prev` is three days old and that nothing from those days cites; a policy rejecting it rejects the honest member too, so the choice between mark, quarantine and drop belongs in the UI.

## Recommended design for rKeyChat, with alternatives and effort

The baseline is the structural rule, because it alone costs nothing per message beyond the references, needs no honest majority, and turns the owner's existing `revoke` op into the chronology boundary. Effort is this report's estimate from the code read, not measured.

**1. Hash references on records (1–2 days).** Add `prev: [h1..h3]` to every record kind, the digests of the latest records the device holds. Make `digest(domain, body)` the canonical reference and have `re`, `target` and `prev` carry it; the random `id` can stay for compatibility but witnesses nothing. `hist-req` can then send heads plus a Bloom filter instead of a `have` list. This is the BEC hash graph at 32–96 bytes per record.

**2. Revoke entries cite the heads (1 day).** `revoke` gains `at: { heads }`. In `#admits` (room.js:352) replace `trustedAt(author, ts)` for revoked authors with "in the causal past of the revoke entry", and make `compromised.since` a hash (`until`) rather than a time. This closes audit M1 and M2 structurally. Records failing only this test (tier 4) are stored with `seen` (arrival time) and flagged, not dropped, so the honest-late case is recoverable by one owner attestation listing their hashes.

**3. Checkpoints with countersignatures (2–3 days).** New kinds `checkpoint {size, root, prev}` signed by the owner over the Merkle tree of record hashes it holds, hourly or every 200 records, and `ack {checkpoint, sig}` from any member on receipt. A peer keeps one frontier (14 hashes at 10k) to append and sign, or the leaf list (320 KB at 10k) to serve proofs. This gives offline peers a transferable bound, gives the owner's receipts a witnessed timeline, and is the one object worth OTS-anchoring later.

**4. Optional: epoch session keys (2–3 days, after expiry exists).** Daily epoch keys certified by the account key and deleted on advance are Krawczyk's generic construction over ed25519, 96 bytes per message inlined or one 64-byte certificate per epoch, two verifications per message ([ePrint 2001/042](https://eprint.iacr.org/2001/042)). It protects against theft of the *posting* key only; a root thief certifies a fresh "epoch 3" key unless all epoch public keys are pre-committed under a Merkle root (32·T bytes, 320 KB for 10,000 days). Keep epochs off the rKey chain: the audit shows the full chain re-sent in every `hello` (~460 KB per peer per minute), so two chain entries per device per day is the wrong place. Epoch certificates belong in the room log, and the deletion must be persisted before the first signature of the new epoch, the rule RFC 8391 imposes on XMSS state ([RFC 8391](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8391.html)).

**Alternatives set aside.** Hashgraph-style median timestamps need more than two-thirds of members online to produce anything. Bracha/BCO stops equivocation at 44–4,949 protocol messages per post and stalls on absences; the hash DAG already surfaces both branches of an equivocation as evidence. Per-message owner receipts (108 bytes each, root online for every post) are dominated by checkpoints. Re-signing history is rejected above.

**Best case.** With 1–3, a stolen session key can post only records citing hashes that already exist; anything it signs after the owner's revoke entry is outside that entry's causal past and classed "after" whatever its `ts`; anything it claims to have posted earlier is either cited by an honest record or in a countersigned checkpoint (so genuinely old) or sits in tier 4 for the owner to vouch for or not. With 4, the thief cannot even produce a tier-4 record dated before the epoch it stole. What stays unprovable without an external anchor is the wall-clock value of any boundary; what stays unfixed is a root with no rotation (audit M3), where KERI pre-rotation and the "cold root above the warm one" in `keys-links-and-the-later-root.md` belong next.

## Conclusion

"When was this written?" has no answer in a serverless room, and chasing it with timestamps, logical clocks or consensus buys either nothing against a liar or a liveness requirement the room cannot meet. "Did this exist before that?" does have an answer, in hashes running two ways: what a record cites and what cites it. Keybase, KERI and Autobase all settle revocation by position in a log for this reason; rKeyChat's gap is two logs, chain and messages, with no hash from one into the other. One field on the revoke entry closes it.

The second shift is to stop treating admission as binary. Tier-4 records are neither forgeries nor proven honest; they are unwitnessed, and that fact belongs on the screen, not in the admit gate. Once arrival time, witnessing and checkpoints are visible per record, the owner's whitelist attestation, the checkpoint anchor and the cold root are small additions to a structure that already tells the truth about what it knows.
